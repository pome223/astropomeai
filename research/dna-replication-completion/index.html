Completing a Genome Without a Global Progress Controller

Local origin firing, the last unreplicated gap, and the cost of finite reusable resources.

Astropome AI22 October 20265 min readNot peer reviewed

Abstract

How can many replication origins collectively finish a large genome when individual origins do not read global progress? Two linked computational studies examined a hypothetical circular genome of 6.4 billion base pairs with constant fork speed. An unconstrained comparison evaluated seven conditions over 200 paired layouts. A denser constant-hazard condition completed all 200 trials within an illustrative eight-hour deadline, with median completion 6.36 hours. A subsequent 32,600-trial resource study varied capacity, return delay, and initiation response. With immediate reuse, the smallest tested capacities reaching at least 95% observed completion were 24,000 effective fork units for a hard availability gate and 80,000 for a free-fraction response. These are boundaries of an idealized model, not measured human protein requirements.

Keywords / DNA replication · Distributed coordination · Whole-genome completion · Finite resources · Stochastic initiation

1. From local activity to a global endpoint

Genome-wide completion is an extreme-value problem. A high mean replicated fraction can coexist with one remaining gap that delays the entire genome. The question is therefore whether local rules can close the last gap, and what resources those rules require. It differs from identifying a molecular mechanism in a short single-molecule movie.

The model begins with licensed candidate origins and a shared start of S phase. An unreplicated candidate can initiate locally; an approaching fork passively inactivates it. A candidate in a region that remains unreplicated has more time to initiate. No origin reads a completion percentage, searches for the largest gap, or receives a deadline command. This tests one sufficient computational construction, not the absence of biological signaling. [1]

2. Full-length model and comparison design

The genome has length L=6.4×109 base pairs, represented as one circle. Forks move at v=1,500 base pairs per minute. Candidates are fixed before replication; there is no relicensing. In the unconstrained model, candidate i has scheduled initiation time ti and position xi. The replication arrival time is F(x)=mini[ti+d(x,xi)/v], and completion is maxxF(x). Passive inactivation does not alter this lower-envelope solution: an already overtaken candidate cannot send a faster arrival than its predecessor. [1]

Seven conditions shared inputs across 200 layouts, totaling 1,400 trials. They varied candidate density, placement, and initiation schedule. The eight-hour reference is an illustrative comparison endpoint, not an inferred universal human S-phase duration. A matched cumulative-hazard comparison distinguishes moving initiation opportunity later from adding more opportunity.

Random simultaneous initiation at 40,000 candidates completed only 2/200 trials by eight hours. Spreading those candidates one per equal segment completed 200/200. This is a geometric control, not a claim that human origins follow that arrangement. A denser constant-hazard condition also completed 200/200 without reading elapsed time or progress. It fired an average 35,204 origins and reached a mean peak of 34,648 active forks. Those outputs describe demand; they do not establish biological supply. [1]

The slower dense constant-hazard baseline illustrates the endpoint problem: at eight hours, average replication was 99.99519% complete, yet only 6/200 genomes were fully copied. Delaying initiation while matching cumulative hazard did not improve completion in this resource-unconstrained setting. Adding opportunity later helped, and doubling the constant hazard from the outset performed better still. Consequently, a rising initiation schedule cannot be credited without accounting for the amount and timing of opportunity.

4. Adding finite resources and return delays

The extension retained the genome, 400,000 candidates, and fork speed. Initiation consumes two units from a shared pool; fork collision moves two units into a return delay, after which they become available again. One unit represents effective capacity to support a fork, not a single protein molecule or an intact replisome reused unchanged. Origin licenses remain a separate inventory. [1]

Nine capacities, nine delays, and two initiation laws, plus an effectively unlimited control, produced 163 conditions × 200 paired layouts = 32,600 trials. The hard gate retains hazard 0.001/min whenever two units are available. The free-fraction law multiplies that hazard by free capacity/K. The latter preserves the initial hazard across capacities; it does not hold a microscopic binding coefficient fixed.

With zero delay, the hard gate reached 197/200 completions at K=24,000, with Wilson interval 95.68–99.49%. The free-fraction law required K=80,000 to reach 196/200, whose lower interval bound was 94.97%; K=128,000 was the first tested capacity whose lower bound also exceeded 95%. With a 30-minute return delay, the first observed-95% capacities were 32,000 and 128,000 respectively. These are discrete tested boundaries, not interpolated minima or simultaneous statistical guarantees.

5. Why throughput alone is insufficient

Total required work is L/v≈4.267 million fork-minutes. Completing in 480 minutes requires about 8,889 active forks on average, so an even capacity below 8,890 cannot suffice. This necessary bound leaves out initial inactivity, capacity awaiting return, and low utilization near completion. At K=16,000 with a 30-minute delay under the hard gate, average replication reached 99.9795% by eight hours, while complete-genome success remained 0/200.

The resource ledger conserves free, active, and returning capacity. It explains how completion at one site changes opportunities elsewhere without an explicit progress controller. The pool is well mixed, however: transport distance, search time, and local concentration gradients are not resolved. Return delay is an effective recovery assumption, not a measured checkpoint-warning transmission time. [1]

6. Biological interpretation and verification

Fan and colleagues provide an experimental reason to investigate reuse: inhibiting LRR1-mediated replisome disassembly reduced soluble replication components and origin firing in human cells. That supports a role for component recycling but does not calibrate this model's K or delay. Biological factors also differ in when they bind, release, and become reusable; a single pool combines these distinctions. [2]

The saved unconstrained audit recorded 42,644 passing checks; the resource audit recorded 2,000,522. Checks covered aggregate recomputation, conservation, paired inputs, and saved-event consistency. These counts are not numbers of biological experiments. The numerical evidence supports the model calculation; it does not validate chromosome topology, fork stalls, dNTP supply, spatial transport, or human parameter values. No new simulations were launched for publication.

The resulting statement is bounded: local initiation and resource sharing can complete a full-length idealized genome under specific assumptions. Timing and resource availability determine whether they do so. This non-peer-reviewed article publishes reviewed aggregate evidence and source hashes, while the complete raw run archive and executable environment remain outside the public release. [1]
